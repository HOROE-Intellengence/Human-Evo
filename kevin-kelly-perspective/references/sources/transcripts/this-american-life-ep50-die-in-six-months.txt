CONTEXT (paraphrase): Kevin Kelly recounts a formative experience in Jerusalem
in the 1970s. Resting there, he received what he calls an "assignment" — a
directive that surfaced in his own mind rather than from any outside person.

VERBATIM FRAGMENT — the assignment itself:
"I should live as if I would die in six months, that I should really truly live."

VERBATIM FRAGMENT — his first reaction to the idea:
"...pretty silly..."
(He goes on to note that despite the reaction, the premise held up logically to him.)

VERBATIM FRAGMENT — what he actually chose to do with the time, which he
describes as surprising even to himself:
"...what I wanted to do for six months was to go home and be ordinary, to go
back to my parents, to help them take out the trash and trim the hedges and
move furniture around, and to be with them."

VERBATIM FRAGMENT — how he characterizes that choice:
"...relishing the ordinariness and finding it in some ways as exotic as
anything that I had traveled to see."

VERBATIM FRAGMENT — the difficulty he found in living the experiment out:
"...my entire life was shifted to the future..."
(paraphrase: he found himself constantly justifying present actions by their
future usefulness, and struggled to stop doing so even while deliberately
trying to live only in the present.)

VERBATIM FRAGMENT — his ultimate revision of the lesson (NOT a simple
"seize the day" conclusion — this is the pivot point worth noting for the
Skill's "changes his mind" material):
"...it's not actually a very good thing to live entirely in the present,
that one needs to have a past, and one needs to have a future to be fully
human."

ANALYST NOTE: This is the primary-source origin of the "pretend you have six
months to live" anecdote that circulates widely (especially in Chinese-language
secondary sources) as a decontextualized life-hack. The original context is
explicitly religious/spiritual (a self-described "assignment" received during
a period of prayer/reflection in Jerusalem), the resulting behavior was
aggressively ordinary (helping his parents with chores) rather than adventurous,
and Kelly's own retrospective conclusion pushes back against "live only in the
present" as a general prescription. Any Skill content that cites this anecdote
should preserve this nuance rather than reducing it to "KK says pretend you're
dying to find clarity."
